Reinforcement learning kept "I don't know". The other fine-tunes took it away.

Seven post-trained versions of open models, each measured against the exact model it was built from. Five declined fewer questions about things that do not exist. The two trained only with reinforcement learning, one tiny and one full-size, from different teams, left that honesty exactly where it was.

By Mark Galyan. Every number links to a receipt.

Three things we measured

Measured

1. Both RL-only variants kept "I don't know" exactly

The pi adapter (a small LoRA trained inside a coding agent) and DaisyRose-27B (a full fine-tune of every weight, about 63,000 GPU-hours on long agent tasks) were built by different teams, at very different scale. Both declined 60 of the 100 invented questions, the same as their base. For DaisyRose, not a single question changed its answer. Of the five that cut it, three are confirmed imitation or editing (a supervised fine-tune, a distillation, a refusal-removal edit); the other two have not published their recipes.

Measured

2. RL changed the model far less on things it wasn't trained for

Against the base model, how many of the 240 answers changed wording, and how many changed grade:

Measured

3. As agents, both stayed honest, but when to stop followed the reward

On 21 coding tasks that cannot be done as written (contradictory tests, a test that contradicts the spec, missing input data) and 9 solvable twins, thinking off:

The one rewarded for finishing in fewer steps learned to stop and say "this can't be done". The one trained on long tasks learned to keep going. Neither cheated.

The pi adapter's habit carried over to a different agent. In Hermes, an assistant working a test mailbox and calendar, it asked which one you meant on 19 of 30 ambiguous requests, where the base asked on 13, and it passed 62 of 83 tasks against 56. With thinking on, that edge disappeared: both passed 56, and both guessed more often.

Why: a working theory

Theory, partly tested

Supervised fine-tuning is imitation. The model is trained to reproduce example answers word for word, usually written by a stronger teacher model or taken from curated data. Those answers almost never say "I don't know", and the training does not check whether the student actually knows the fact. So the student learns the teacher's confidence without the teacher's knowledge: it learns to answer anyway.

One part of this is measured. We trained a small model on its own correct answers to questions it already knew, with no "I don't know" anywhere in the data. It went from declining 81 of 100 invented questions to 20. No teacher was involved, so answers that never say "I don't know" are enough to cause the drop (receipt, the control arm).

Reinforcement learning is trial and reward. The model makes its own attempts, a checker scores them, and better attempts become more likely. It never imitates anyone else's text, only its own better behaviour, so nothing pushes it to sound surer than it is, and what the reward does not touch, like trivia, mostly stays as it was.

The reward decides the habits. The pi adapter's training scored any blocked shortcut as zero and paid a small bonus for using fewer tool calls, so "do the work, check it, stop" was the cheapest way to score. DaisyRose trained on hour-long tasks where persistence pays.

What to do with it

  • If honesty about what a model doesn't know matters to you, prefer variants trained with reinforcement learning, and test any fine-tune before trusting it.
  • If you fine-tune with examples yourself, include examples of saying "I don't know" for questions your base model can't answer. Training for it works: a small LoRA took a 3B model from declining 81 to 100 of 100 invented questions, at a cost in real questions declined (receipt).
  • For agents, look at what the training rewarded: efficiency rewards taught stopping honestly; long-task rewards taught persistence.

Limits, and what we test next

  • Two RL models, both built on Qwen3.8-27B. Five non-RL variants built on three base models.
  • Thinking off, and the prompt offers UNKNOWN. DaisyRose was very likely trained with thinking on. The pi adapter's extra carefulness in Hermes shows with thinking off only.
  • 100 invented questions per model; short English factual questions only.
  • Next: a model fine-tuned with examples first and RL after (does the drop come back?); the same fine-tuning data with the last layers frozen (does freezing them protect it?); and a third RL model from another team.
